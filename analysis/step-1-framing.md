# Step 1 — Frame the Question (Days 1–2) ✅

## 1. The one-sentence question (draft — [Company] filled in Step 2)

> **"Is [Company] over-staffed or under-productive versus sector peers, and what EBITDA value is at stake from closing the gap?"**

Teaching note: a good framing question has three properties — it forces a **yes/no-ish answer**, it embeds the **comparison** (versus peers), and it names the **money** (value at stake). "Organisational effectiveness is the ability to make and implement good decisions quickly at all levels" (Humatica's definition) — our proxy for that, using only public data, is staffing productivity.

## 2. Issue tree (MECE)

```
Q: Is [Company] over-staffed or under-productive vs peers, and what's the value at stake?
│
├── A. PRODUCTIVITY GAP — target generates less output per employee
│     ├── Rev per FTE vs peer median / IQR
│     └── Trend: 3-yr CAGR of Rev per FTE vs peers
│
├── B. SCALE EFFECT — target is smaller, so gap is structural, not a failure
│     ├── Rev per FTE within same revenue band (scale-adjusted benchmarking)
│     └── Gross-margin/business-mix differences between target and peers
│
├── C. DELIBERATE OVER-INVESTMENT — target invests in people ahead of revenue
│     ├── Headcount growth vs revenue growth (hiring ahead of the curve)
│     └── Staff cost per FTE vs peers (paying more ≠ having more)
│
└── D. DATA ARTEFACT — the gap is an illusion of the filings
      ├── Abridged accounts (no turnover disclosed) for any peer
      ├── Period-end misalignment / year-end changes / restatements
      └── UK small-company regime changes altering disclosure over time
```

**Teaching note — MECE:** Mutually Exclusive (branches don't overlap) + Collectively Exhaustive (together they cover all explanations). Branch D is what separates a careful analyst from a naive one: if any peer files abridged accounts, the "peer median" is polluted and every downstream number is wrong.

## 3. Three falsifiable hypotheses (with kill tests)

A hypothesis is only useful if data can prove it **wrong**. Each hypothesis below states what number would kill it.

**H1 — Productivity gap:** "[Company]'s revenue per FTE is >25% below the peer median, driven by lower output per employee rather than higher pay."
- *Kill test:* Rev/FTE within 10% of peer median → gap doesn't exist.
- *Kill test:* staff cost per FTE is also >25% below median → it's a pay/quality mix issue, not productivity.

**H2 — Scale-adjusted persistence:** "The gap survives when comparing only against peers of similar revenue scale (within ±50% of target revenue)."
- *Kill test:* gap shrinks to <5 pts of staff-cost ratio within the band → it was a scale effect (Branch B), not under-performance.

**H3 — Growth divergence:** "[Company]'s revenue CAGR (3-yr) trails the peer median by ≥5 pts, so the productivity gap is structural, not a temporary investment phase."
- *Kill test:* target's revenue growth ≥ peer median AND headcount growth ≤ revenue growth → company is hiring productively (Branch C); the "value at stake" answer becomes "no forced action" — a legitimate consulting answer.

## 4. Day-1 answer (Pyramid Principle drill)

Write the answer you'd give *today*, knowing it will change:
> "Preliminary answer: [Company] appears under-productive rather than over-staffed; the value at stake is roughly [gap %] of staff costs, pending data collection (Days 4–7)."

**Teaching note:** consultants answer first, then support. Rewriting your Day-1 answer on Day 14 shows the audience how evidence moved the conclusion — that's the story you tell in the walkthrough.
